The Ledger of the Golden Sheaf

An archival expedition in which almost nothing goes as promised. IA 280 Research Methods.

Where I've been

    The conspiracy is made up. The archives are real.

    There is no Order of the Golden Sheaf, and there is no lost ledger. Every archive in this game is real, though. The catalog screens were built from the real catalog in August 2026 and checked against it again in October, not invented.

    What's inside the folders is a different story. Nobody who worked on this game has sat in that reading room and read them, so the memo on page 26, the projector letters, and everything in the side threads are made up. So are two entries in the folder lists: the stray folder in Miscellany and the second J. G. Harrar. They're there to show you the kinds of things that archives hold. Use this game for how archives work, and not for what Harrar actually wrote or what his files actually contain.

    • Rockefeller Foundation records: 7,522.48 cubic feet, 1906-2017 (bulk 1924-1990), in 28 record groups. As of October 2026, none of those record groups was flagged in the catalog as having content available online.
    • J. George Harrar papers: 21.57 cubic feet, 1906-1982 (bulk 1958-1982), in four series: Correspondence; Speeches, Manuscripts and Publications; Miscellany; and photographs.
    • Series 1 has five subseries: Outgoing, Confidential, General, Collateral Interests, and Relating to Written Materials. The Confidential subseries holds 23 folders described only by date.
    • The access note really does say "Open for research." That's the trick the whole game turns on.
    • Access: you need an appointment, booked at least three days ahead. The reading room is open Monday to Friday, 9:30 to 5:00. It's pencils and laptops only (no pens), and one folder at a time with an OUT card. Photography is generally permitted. Material stored off-site can take up to ten business days to retrieve.
    • Digitization on request: reference scans are free and capped at 10 requests per researcher per calendar year, and the Center "endeavors to fulfill all digitization requests within 120 business days."

    To be precise about one thing: the Rockefeller Archive Center does digitize on request, and it does host some material online. The narrow, true claim this game makes is that these record groups aren't sitting on the web waiting to be read.

    And one more honest note. The real finding aid says the Harrar papers mostly cover his activities outside the Foundation, and that the Confidential files were originally labeled "Confidential" or "Personal." Whether anything like that memo is in there is exactly the kind of thing a finding aid can't tell you. You'd have to go and look.

    Where the disasters come from

    The things that go wrong aren't invented, either. Most of them happen, in nearly this form, in three pages of:

    Jensenius, F. R. (2014). The fieldwork of quantitative data collection. PS: Political Science & Politics, 47(2), 402-404.

    • The credential loop, where you need an entry pass to meet the person who issues entry passes.
    • Gatekeeping with no procedure behind it, decided on the spot.
    • Five days of being sent from official to official, when the real reason for the refusal was a fear that she would find out the data were poor quality.
    • Being refused because a previous researcher broke the rules.
    • Records that never existed, then were never archived, then had burned, and were later found, unrecognized, in another archive. Her rule: always look for yourself.
    • A kindly pre-compiled table in which many figures were simply wrong.
    • The spreadsheet in which every row read 100%.

    Jensenius cites Brooke Harrington on the social psychology of access. That's the same Harrington we'll read for the ethnography session. Getting in is a problem whatever kind of data you're after.

    The side threads (the film cans, the Facebook group, the garage, the country house, and the mate) are borrowed, with the details changed, from things that happened to colleagues of mine who work in archives.

    No network requests. Nothing leaves your browser, except for the one moment when the game asks you to go and look something up yourself.
